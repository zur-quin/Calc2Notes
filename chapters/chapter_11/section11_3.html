11.3.1. Integral Test

In most cases, we cannot easily determine the actual value that a series converges to. However, we can often determine whether a series is convergent or divergent. In this section, we will begin to investigate methods used to determine whether or not a series is convergent.

Solution

The terms in the series have the sequence

(𝑎𝑛)𝑛=1∞=(11,12,13,14=12,15,16,…)

leading us to the sequence of partial sums

(𝑠𝑛)𝑛=1∞=(1,1+12,1+12+13,…)

and this is just gross and difficult to work with.

With the harmonic series we could do some clever bounding and algebra, but those are typically difficult techniques to apply in general. Instead let’s see if we can approach this from a different angle.

Observe we can “graph” the sequence like a step function. We can construct a bunch of rectangles of width 1 and height 𝑎𝑛=1𝑛

and the area of all the rectangles is the series

∑𝑛=1∞1𝑛.

A lower bound for the area under this curve is the integral of the function that passes through the left end points of our rectangles: 𝑦=1𝑥! (not factorial, just excitement)

We can use the convergence or divergence of improper integrals that directly correspond to the series.

In this case, we already know that ∫1∞1𝑥d𝑥 diverges since it is of the form ∫1∞1𝑥𝑝d𝑥 with 𝑝≤1.

However, visually we can see that ∑𝑛=1∞1𝑛>∫1∞1𝑥d𝑥, since the area of each of the rectangles is like a left Reimann sum for the integral and the function is decreasing.

Thus, ∑𝑛=1∞1𝑛 diverges.

Notice ∑𝑛=1∞1𝑛2<∫1∞1𝑥2d𝑥. Since ∫1𝑥2d𝑥 is of the form ∫1∞1𝑥𝑝d𝑥 and 𝑝>1, this integral converges, so the series

∑𝑛=1∞1𝑛2

converges.

Warning

  • This does not tell you what value the series converges to!!!!!!
  • This test does not always hold without all 3 of the assumptions. 𝑓 must be continuous, positive, and decreasing to use this test. If you don’t check these conditions you can’t use the integral test.

11.3.2. Remainder Estimate for Integral Test

Suppose a series converges, and we want to estimate its value. How can we do this?

  • If a series converges, then, by definition (even if it is hard for us to calculate directly), its value is the limit of the sequence of partial sums. So, we can take a sufficiently long partial sum to approximate the value. The larger 𝑛 we use for the 𝑛th partial sum, the better the estimate, since we’re adding more of the terms.

Solution

𝑠10=∑𝑛=1101𝑛2=1+14+19+…+1100≈1.54976773117

To guarantee our approximation is accurate to at least 3 decimal places, we need the error (or the remainder 𝑅𝑛) to be less than 0.001. We’ll see what value of 𝑛 guarantees 𝑅𝑛≤0.001 by solving when 𝑅𝑛<0.001. We know by the remainder estimate that

𝑅𝑛≤∫𝑛∞1𝑥2d𝑥

This integral is

∫𝑛∞1𝑥2d𝑥=lim𝑡→∞∫𝑛𝑡1𝑥2d𝑥=lim𝑡→∞−1𝑥|𝑛𝑡=lim𝑡→∞1𝑛−1𝑡=1𝑛.

Then, since we know 𝑅𝑛≤1𝑛, to get 𝑅𝑛<0.001=11000 we need 1𝑛<11000 or 𝑛>1000. So, to guarantee that the estimate is accurate up to 3 decimal places we need to use 𝑛>1000.

It turns out this is an overestimate of what is actually needed to get an accurate approximation of the series. We only need up to 𝑛=32 to get 3 decimals of accuracy in this case. Much less than 1000.
