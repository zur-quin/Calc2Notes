7.4. Integration by Partial Fractions

7.4a Learning Objectives

  • I can understand how to rewrite rational functions as a sum of partial fractions.
  • I can understand when and how to integrate by partial fractions.

7.4.1. Motivation

How can we rewrite the function 𝑓(𝑥)=1𝑥−4−1𝑥−5?

𝑓(𝑥)=1𝑥−4−1𝑥−5=𝑥−5(𝑥−4)(𝑥−5)−𝑥−4(𝑥−4)(𝑥−5)=𝑥−5−𝑥+4(𝑥−4)(𝑥−5)=−1(𝑥−4)(𝑥−5)

Which is easier to take the integral of:

1𝑥−4−1𝑥−5 or −1(𝑥−4)(𝑥−5)?

The difference on the left is easier to take the integral of.

So, if we are asked to integrate the expression on the right, it would be nice to rewrite it as the expression on the left first. This process of rewriting a rational function as a sum of “simpler” rational functions is called partial fraction decomposition.

7.4.2. Partial Fraction Decomposition

Idea:

Say 𝑓(𝑥)=𝑃(𝑥)𝑄(𝑥) where 𝑃 and 𝑄 are polynomials. Then, we can factor the denominator, 𝑄(𝑥), into its linear and quadratic terms:

𝑄(𝑥)=(𝑎1𝑥+𝑏1)…(𝑎𝑛𝑥+𝑏𝑛)(𝐴1𝑥2+𝐵1𝑥+𝐶1)…(𝐴𝑚𝑥2+𝐵𝑚𝑥+𝐶𝑚)

Then we can write 𝑓(𝑥) as:

𝑓(𝑥)=𝑆(𝑥)+𝐽1𝑎1𝑥+𝑏1+…+𝐽2𝑎𝑛𝑥+𝑏𝑛+𝐾1𝐴1𝑥2+𝐵1𝑥+𝐶1+…+𝐽2𝐴𝑚𝑥2+𝐵𝑚𝑥+𝐶𝑚

where 𝑆(𝑥) is a polynomial.

More precisely, the steps you can use are as follows.

Tip

General process for decomposing a rational function into its partial fractions.

  1. If 𝑓(𝑥)=𝑃(𝑥)𝑄(𝑥) is not proper (deg𝑃(𝑥)≥deg𝑄(𝑥)) then use polynomial long division to find

    𝑓(𝑥)=𝑆(𝑥)+𝑅(𝑥)𝑄(𝑥), with deg𝑅(𝑥)<deg𝑄(𝑥)
  2. Then, if 𝑅(𝑥)𝑄(𝑥) cannot be integrated as is, factor 𝑄(𝑥) into linear and quadratic irreducible terms.

  3. Split 𝑅(𝑥)𝑄(𝑥) into its sum of partial fractions with unknown numerators.

  4. Solve for the numerators.

    1. Set original fraction equal to the sum of partial fractions.
    2. Multiply both sides by 𝑄(𝑥).
    3. Solve for unknown constants using a system of equations and/or plugging in some 𝑥 values.

7.4.3. Integrals Needing Partial Fraction Decomposition

Solution

First we factor and simplify everything we can

∫2𝑥3−𝑥2+4𝑥𝑥4+4𝑥2d𝑥=∫𝑥(2𝑥2−𝑥+4)𝑥2(𝑥2+4)d𝑥=∫2𝑥2−𝑥+4𝑥(𝑥2+4)d𝑥

Then we find the partial fraction decomposition of the integrand. Since the 𝑥2+4 factor has discriminant 02−4(1)(4)=−16<0 it is irreducible. Then, the form of the partial fraction decomposition is

2𝑥2−𝑥+4𝑥(𝑥2+4)=𝐴𝑥+𝐵𝑥+𝐶𝑥2+4

Multiplying by the denominator gives us

(2𝑥2−𝑥+4)=(𝑥(𝑥2+4))𝐴𝑥+(𝑥(𝑥2+4))𝐵𝑥+𝐶𝑥2+4(2𝑥2−𝑥+4)=(𝑥2+4)𝐴+𝑥(𝐵𝑥+𝐶)

We need a slightly new strategy for 𝐵,𝐶 since the roots of 𝑥2+4 are complex, we will use this strategy for finding 𝐴 also, but it isn’t necessary. Multiplying things out

(2𝑥2−𝑥+4)=(𝑥2+4)𝐴+𝑥(𝐵𝑥+𝐶)2𝑥2−𝑥+4=𝐴𝑥2+4𝐴+𝐵𝑥2+𝐶𝑥2𝑥2−𝑥+4=(𝐴+𝐵)𝑥2+𝐶𝑥+4𝐴

Now lining up like powers on both sides we see that

2𝑥2−𝑥+4=(𝐴+𝐵)𝑥2+𝐶𝑥+4𝐴⇒−1=𝐶

Then we see

2𝑥2−𝑥+4=(1+𝐵)𝑥2+𝐶𝑥+4𝐴⇒1=𝐴

and lastly,

2𝑥2−𝑥+4=(𝐴+𝐵)𝑥2+𝐶𝑥+4𝐴⇒2=𝐴+𝐵⇒2=1+𝐵⇒1=𝐵.

So then the integral becomes

∫2𝑥2−𝑥+4𝑥(𝑥2+4)=∫𝐴𝑥d𝑥+∫𝐵𝑥+𝐶𝑥2+4d𝑥=∫1𝑥d𝑥+∫1𝑥−1𝑥2+4d𝑥=∫1𝑥d𝑥+∫𝑥𝑥2+4d𝑥−∫1𝑥2+4d𝑥

We can solve these integrals with either known antiderivatives or 𝑢-substitution.

∫1𝑥d𝑥=ln|𝑥|+𝐶

And with 𝑢=𝑥2 and some factoring we have

∫1𝑥2+4d𝑥=∫1(4)((𝑥2)2+1)d𝑥=14∫1𝑢2+12d𝑢=12∫1𝑢2+1d𝑢=12∫1𝑢2+1d𝑢=12arctan(𝑢)+𝐶=12arctan(𝑥2)+𝐶

and with 𝑢=𝑥2+4, d𝑢=2𝑥d𝑥 we have

∫𝑥𝑥2+4d𝑥=12∫1𝑢d𝑢=12ln|𝑢|+𝐶=12ln|𝑥2+4|+𝐶

Thus,

∫2𝑥2−𝑥+4𝑥(𝑥2+4)=∫1𝑥d𝑥+∫𝑥𝑥2+4d𝑥−∫1𝑥2+4d𝑥=ln|𝑥|+12ln|𝑥2+4|−12arctan(𝑥2)+𝐶.
